An Investigation Into Consciousness and Its End

philosophy of Mind
This was an essay I wrote as a final project for a class about death and dying. It goes over different theories of consciousness and my thoughts on them. However the analysis is dated and my thoughts will be better articulated in later essays.
Published

November 28, 2025

“Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not.”

— Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus

On a gray November Evening, I laced up my shoes and went for a run, a common enough occurrence to be sure. Though this was a more somber and introspective affair, one usually relegated to a fireplace and an old leather bound book. Something was different about today, and it wasn’t the cold that necessitated me bundle up nor the fact that I didn’t bring my earbuds so as not to have any extradiegetic. When I reached that squat, hut-like structure, I stopped my watch. Walking through the cracked asphalt with little blades of grass stretching to the heavens on a crisp November evening, I sought wisdom from the Oakwood Cemetery. Being in there for a moment, it became painfully obvious that I had never done something like this before. How could someone who thinks of themselves as well versed in the language of mortality have never taken a sojourn such as this. The knowledge was not new per say but it struck a cord, one that reverberated through me like Maria Callas’s voice when at the zenith of singing Casta diva. I felt slightly uneasy, something I couldn’t place my finger on. There was almost a haze that covered the frame, as if the partition between this world and the next was blurry. Headstones of all sizes littered the floor like the ruins of a civilisation long forgotten. I was to dawn the anthropologist’s hat, and see what I could find. The grounds looked jerry rigged together, the way Dr. Frankenstein must have made his creation. I squatted down and read the first one that struck my eye. It was glazed with droplets and there were little green mosses making a home on its cold surface. 

I came to the cemetery to learn of my fate, I came asking the same questions everyone laid to rest there already had. I wanted to learn how to live in the knowing that one day, my outlet to everything I’ve ever known will close forever. But perhaps this is a bad place to start, before I can say what I learned, there is some housekeeping we must attend to. So join me in an exploration of what it means to live and what it means to die.

What are you? Maybe this is an easy question, so let’s attempt to answer it in kind. If I ask you to look into a mirror and point to yourself, you will point at a fleshbag with five appendages (six if you’re a man) on it and identify that thing as ‘me’. So, are you your body? Well if I cut off your hand, are you still you? Most would agree that you’re not any less ‘you’ for being amputated. How far can we take this? Well we could cut off all of your limbs, but why stop there? Lets just take away everything except what makes you tick, your brain. In this thought experiment we can say that everything necessary to keep you ‘alive’ is given to you in this little brain vat. We can suspend you in a jello, and we can give you tools that work as eyes, we can give you a speaker to talk and a microphone to hear. You can expand this as far as you like, what are we left with? You guessed it, a human body, this examination shows us that we already are little brains in vats (fleshy ones at that). But of course we don’t think of ourselves in this way, not that this matters for our investigation. So we have isolated an organ about 2.9-3.1Lb in heft, as you. 

It was once believed that brain size was linked to intelligence, this was known as craniology. There was a famous rivalry known to science as the ‘bone wars’ after two paleontologists (Edward Drinker Cope and Othniel Charles Marsh) who fought over fossils. They are the ones who discovered most dinosaurs, but their rivalry extended past bones and into all scientific pursuits, like macho men they fought over who had the larger brain and therefore the superior intellect. This is now understood to be pseudoscience, brain size is not correlated with intellect. In fact, this so-called science disproves itself by the neat little fact that Einstein’s brain was only 2.7Lb, making it below average. If we are going to say you are something physical then we are relegated to saying you are your brain. But that doesn’t feel right either. When you think of yourself there is no Basal ganglia, Hypothalamus, or Olfactory bulb. Can we go further than Telencephalon and company? Afterall, it wasn’t so long ago that leading minds believed in ‘a ghost in the machine’. We feel in some way distinct from our brains. 

In The Ethics Of Ambiguity, Simon De Bovoar makes a distinction between man as subject and man as object. If we close our eyes and use our imaginations we can transport ourselves to a world beyond the physical. I have many a time been lost in thought for hours, only to be brought back down to earth by an urge that De Bovoar would say comes from the objective part of man. This urge could be the need to pee, hunger, fatigue or an urge to move. We could think of the objective as an anker that keeps the balloon that is the subject from floating free. This tension is what De Bovoar called the ambiguity of man. These two feel different, so let’s treat them as such. Let’s call the subjective, ‘consciousness’. There is as of yet no mechanistic understanding of what consciousness is. In fact David Chalmers believes that we are not even making any progress on this front. In his 1996 book, The Conscious Mind, he makes the distinction between what he calls the easy problems of consciousness and the hard problem of consciousness. The easy problems of consciousness involve explaining how the brain performs functions like perception, memory, and attention, while the hard problem is explaining why and how these processes are accompanied by subjective experience (the feeling of ‘what it’s like’ to be conscious). Given this handicap, we must get creative with our search. 

First we will take the famous definition given by philosopher Thomas Nagel in his landmark paper, What Is It Like To Be A Bat?. “The subjective character of experience” (Nagel, 1974). In other words, a being is conscious if and only if there is something it is like to be that being. I will not go into the derivation of the argument for time, but I would point the reader to the paper for more on his theory. Lucky for us, there is no shortage of theories on what consciousness is, let us examine a handful. 

A rather famous one comes to us from a famous 17th century mathematician, Rene Descartes; called dualism. This is the belief that there are two things that make up the world, matter stuff and soul stuff. In his 1641 Meditations on First Philosophy, he argues that we are soul stuff puppeting our fleshy automata. There were people who thought they could prove this scientifically. In 1907, Dr. Duncan MacDougall weighed dying patients at the moment of death to see if the body lost mass, he interpreted this lost mass as the soul leaving the body. He claimed to find a consistent loss of 21 grams, which later became the famous “weight of the soul.” Today, there is no serious thinker who claims to believe in dualism, why? The objection is called the interaction problem. Or otherwise put, how does the nonphysical soul stuff interact with the physical matter stuff. There is no satisfactory answer to this question and so proponents of soul stuff have had to take a different approach known as property dualism. In this view we call Descartes’s belief, substance dualism. Because there are two substances that make up reality. This new form of dualism is known as property dualism. Claiming that there is only one kind of matter but it exhibits certain kinds of properties, call them metaphysical properties. What these properties are, how they work or why they exist is not well defined and usually left up to the dualist in question. 

Monism offers a radical departure from the centuries-old problem of mind and matter. Unlike dualism, which struggles to explain how a nonphysical soul could influence the tangible world, monism insists on a singular underlying reality. For physicalist monists, consciousness emerges from the intricate dance of neurons and the electrical symphony of the brain, a natural phenomenon that, in principle, can be fully understood. Idealist monists turn the lens inward, claiming that the world itself is constructed within consciousness, making mind primary and matter secondary. Even neutral monism suggests a deeper substrate, a fundamental “stuff” that manifests as both mental and physical phenomena. In each case, monism sidesteps the paradoxes of dualism, yet the enigma of subjective experience persists. Each of these kinds of monisms can be attacked in different ways. For time we will look at physicalist monism. Take a scientist, call her Mary. Mary is the best neuroscientist there is. She has solved all of the easy problems of consciousness. She knows what every neuron does and how every kind of stimulation affects the system on macro and micro scale. In fact she’s especially interested in color, a phenomenon she has never seen. On her 40’th birthday, her eyesight will instantly switch to color vision. The question is, does Marry experience something new about the color red? We are supposing that she knows everything about how the mind perceives red, yet our instinct is to say that she does gain something new from seeing the color for the first time. This thingness, we can call ‘qualia’. From the latin meaning “of what sort” or “of what kind”. (The term was first introduced by the philosopher C.I. Lewis in 1929 during discussions of sense-data theory) This subjective experience of being doesn’t feel like something physical and so any theory must reckon with this strangeness.

Panpsychism is the philosophical view that consciousness is a fundamental and universal feature of the universe. This means that consciousness is a property woven into the fabric of reality, much like the four fundamental forces. As a consequence of this view, ‘It’s turtles all the way down’. Relating this back to our definition, this means that there is something it is like to be an electron. Perhaps this sounds unrealistic, but to not strawman the argument it is important to note that whatever it is like to be an electron would be extremely basic. It wouldn’t even be an understanding of ‘I was there but now I’m here’ the attraction and repulsion itself would be the experience. Aside from this being nonintuitive, there is what is called the combination problem. That being, how do these little consciousness’s give rise to a large one like a fly, dog, or human. 

There is a very interesting theory called The Bicameral Mind theory of consciousness, proposed by Julian Jaynes in his 1976 The Origin Of Consciousness In The Breakdown Of The Bicameral Mind. In it, he makes the radical yet incredibly interesting claim that ancient humans were not ‘conscious’ in the way we are today. Their brains were bicameral ‘two-chambered’. One hemisphere (typically the right) generated auditory hallucinations, experienced as commands from gods or authoritative figures like the king. The other hemisphere (typically the left) obeyed these commands without introspective thought. In other words, early humans heard voices telling them what to do, rather than consciously making decisions. This theory is extremely speculative and as evidence used the Iliad and Odyssey. In his 2006 book The God Delusion, Richard Dawkins described Jaynes’s work as “either complete rubbish or a work of consummate genius” (Dawkins, 2006). We can craft a story around this to make it seem more plausible. We can imagine that there were these internal nudges, leading the individual to do one thing over another. As our minds grew, and language evolved in complexity, these nudges took the form of words. Having no other way to conceive of them, Gods were created to explain these nudges. Eventually, with more development across many domains, there was a gradual attribution of these voices to the self. This would explain why there are people who claim to hear the voice of their God speak to them. What today would be called an auditory hallucination could simply be old software interacting with a new paradigm, causing a disconnect. While this is a very interesting theory, that I must admit, would be cool if true. We have no actual evidence to support it. 

As for myself I take the position that consciousness arises as a result of a complex system and will eventually be explained by science. My position is that consciousness evolved much like the eye or flight, that is to say in a gradient over time. Having internal states like hunger, thirst, lust or pain will motivate an organism to move to a goal which is useful for reproduction. Without some fear, an animal might just sleep in the middle of a field and get eaten. But evolution can do better, if you give an organism an understanding of the world around it, said organism can better interact with its environment to further fuel its reproduction. This map of reality doesn’t even have to match the terrain, as long as it’s useful for reproduction. To prove this we can look at the smell of rotten eggs or spoiled milk. These things smell rancid to us, but great to certain flies and other organisms. Why? Because there is no ‘objective smell’ to anything. It just so happens that if you have a mutation that gives you the unfortunate belief that these things smell good, you will die and not pass down the ‘rotten eggs smell good’ gene.  But now that these organisms have gained this, call it ‘primordial awareness’ they will begin to form aims of their own as a natural byproduct of being given this ability. A perfect illustration of this point goes as follows. Evolution wanted us to reproduce so it gave us huge dopamine, serotonin, oxytocin and adrenalin dumps when we entertain these desires. It worked, in fact, it worked so well that humans want sex for sex sake, rather than the intended outcome (desire reproduction) we hacked our biology to reward us without reproduction by inventing birth control. This can be extrapolated to all facets of life, with the idea being that humans became the first and maybe only organisms who were true generalists. Where I mean generalists about goals. Evolution didn’t intend for us to discover leptons, take pictures of black holes or build the empire state building. But because of the tools it gave us, we were able to extrapolate to all of these wonderful things.

When looking at other animals, it is obvious that humans have horrible instincts. We are practically useless until puberty, and it takes us over 20 years to reach full maturation. This, in contrast to Zibras, who can walk minutes after birth. Our superpower is our capacity to learn and create our own ends, separate from the ones preprogrammed into us.

Lets now turn to some neuroscience, to explore how strange consciousness really is. The first hemispherectomy (surgical removal of one cerebral hemisphere) was performed in 1928 by Walter Dandy. In the following years this was done to help children suffering from seizures. These people were literally missing half their brain and seemed to be operating just fine. In 1960, a former World War II paratrooper known to science only as W.J. was suffering from extreme, uncontrollable epileptic seizures. These seizures were so violent that they crossed from one hemisphere of his brain to the other through a thick neural bridge called the corpus callosum, setting off electrical storms across his whole brain. He was given a procedure, called a corpus callosotomy at the California Institute of Technology Medical Center by Roger Sperry and his young graduate student Michael Gazzaniga. These men decided to conduct experiments of the seemingly normal W.J. Their findings were nothing short of astonishing. It seemed that W.J.’s consciousness had been bifurcated in two. Resulting in a real life Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde situation. This springs to mind the gedonken experiment of taking two halves of a person’s brain and plopping them into two bodies. Would they both be that person? But the weirdness doesn’t stop here. Let’s take two such brain disorders. 

Anton-Babinski Syndrome is a rare neurological condition where a person is cortically blind (their brain cannot process visual information) but they insist that they can see, even though they obviously cannot. This denial of blindness is called anosognosia. It’s one of the clearest demonstrations that the brain can be wrong about its own internal states. 

In 1985 a British musicologist and conductor named Clive Wearing suffered a severe herpes simplex encephalitis infection that destroyed parts of his brain, including the hippocampus, which is essential for forming new memories. He became known as the man with the seven second memory. Sort of like the famous H.M. that every first year psychology student learns about, but Wearing seemed to have more lucidity than this. You can find eerie journal entries that read “I am awake for the first time” only to be crossed off with “No, this is me.” or even one that jumps out of the page in big bold letters “I do live!!!”. What are we to make of this? Is Clive the same man each time he writes these things? To us looking from the outside, it would be very simple to answer yes. But as I have just shown, he would disagree. How do you know that you are the same person you were yesterday? “I have all of the same memories as I did before. I act the same.” Sleep is known as the cousin of death, for those few hours, you tasted oblivion. Does the conscious experience you call ‘me’ persist through this oblivion? Let’s say I give you a piece of paper and tell you to write your ‘statement of being’ where I mean, your declaration of existence. But then erased your memory of having written it. Objectively, we know what happened, you wrote this statement on the paper. But in a subjective sense, did you ever do it? I suspect the answer might be no. 

Let’s continue this, we’ve been asking, “what you are?” But is the ‘you’ we are talking about even real? Through my own philosophical and scientific investigations I have come to the opinion that the ‘self’ is not real. But extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. From the moment we come into the world we are talked to/of as a self, a conscious agent. But we know many things to have been learned. A perfect example is a gestalt, this is a form of optical illusion that exploits the shortcuts that the brain uses to construct an image of the world around it. The brain is a very efficient prediction machine, AI researchers would die for a model as energy efficient as the human brain. When the brain sees a gestalt, it infers what must be missing. This is because it is predicting what the object should be based on previous experience. Soccer (Football) is the most popular sport in the world. Most humans know what a soccer ball looks like. Therefore a soccer ball gestalt can trick people into thinking that they are looking at a 3-D shape with a circular outline. But when shown to tribes people who have not been exposed to soccer balls, the necessary heuristics do not exist for the illusion to work. The self is much the same way. Look at most languages, they are littered with self referential descriptors (me, my, mine, he, his, theirs) Our language forces us into these patterns of thinking. Let’s go beyond this, let’s explain this evolutionarily. Of course, as Andrew Huberman would say “We were not consulted in the design faze.” So the picture I am going to paint is my best educated guess. As was stated before, consciousness evolved via gradient, not all at once or in quantized discrete chunks. You have a bunch of cells that are united by shared DNA and that are interested in replication. In the tribe, let’s take two people, Uhg, and Orc. Both are males who want to gain the highest share of females for reproduction. There is something special about Orc, due to a random mutation, he has started to think of himself as a ‘he’. He has become aware of himself as being an individual who desires ends. Ends that contradict those of Uhg. One day, after a hunt, Orc begins to boast about how he got the killing blow. He begins to play politics, taking the lion share of the credit for himself as often as possible. As a result, getting to reproduce more than Uhg. He is behaving in his own self interest like every animal before him. But the difference is that his understanding of his self interest has become more advanced than that of his competition. I have made a lot of claims, but how can this be shown? All that is needed to really see this is long form meditation. If one does this for long enough they can reach a state of seeing that there is no observer. You just are the arising of the thoughts, ego deaths via meditation, fasting and the ever reliable psychedelics make this observation very clear. For more on this idea, I recommend The Case Against Reality (Hoffman, 2019), Why Buddhism Is True (Wright, 2017) and the writings of Sam Harris.

There are volumes more that I could write about this strange thing we are calling consciousness. But I fear it is time for us to return to mortality. There will be a time in the future where the thing that you are will cease to be. This thought has terrified us for as long as we have been able to conceive of it. The very first recorded story, The Epic Of Gilgamesh deals with mortality and the nonpermanence of life. Aristotle writes to us that you never step in the same river twice. Change is inevitable, once your cells reach the Hayflick limit (40-60 mitotic divisions of somatic cells) there are no more telomeres to snip and your DNA no longer gets faithfully copied, leading to some kind of organ failure. Ideally we would be able to manipulate telomerase so that we could regrow your telomeres. This way you could insure neurodegenerative diseases that become expressed after ‘n’ number of divisions, never get the chance to express themselves. Or augment ourselves with technology, such as a mechanical heart, which has already successfully been done. But there is no promise that this will happen in our lifetimes. And even if it does, it will be our designer babies who will have the best shot at eternity, since we will be able to optimize their genes in vitro rather than an ad hoc in vivo intervention.

So if it is our fate to die, and there is not even a hope of understanding what we are. What is one to do? Well, perhaps we ask those who have already passed on. Now it is time to return to that chilly November day. Let's go back to the crisp feeling of our nose and fingers. To that slightly yellow grass and crunchy leaves. What story can I glean from my species’ way of honoring its dead? I chose to look at what was written on the stones for clues. I took pictures of many.

 Father Jay Tayloor had his picture printed on the stone in 1960. That was interesting to see. He was like a ghost, his eyes were looking out when man first walked on the moon, or when the Berlin wall fell. He must have seen that cemetery filled with little American flags after 9/11 or the number of stones swell when the largest world wide pandemic swept the globe since the Spanish flew, which he lived through.

Mark and Stewart Boland, died one year apart in 2020 and 2021 both had a music sheet carved into their stones.

Twin brothers, Adam and Bryan Madrigal seemed to have died at a young age on July 14, 1985. “In memory of our little angels” was written, and two praying baby angels in the charly brown art style are carved on the sides. 

The Hopkins family bought  a large stone for generations. They divided their stone into two halves, one for fathers and one for mothers. The most recent inductee to the Hopkins hall of fame is David P. who died on August 1st of 2020, his name was written on the mothers side for lack of space though it seems little stones with three initials, ending in ‘H.’ have started to be placed at the altar of the stone. David P. Hopkins was born on July 4th, 1928. That would make the headstone, 43 years old when he was born. The first name on that stone was William C. Hopkins, who died on December 7th, 1884. I wonder what William C. Hopkins was like, I wonder what he did and who his ancestors were. I was looking at these people through less than a peep hole. If there is a term for experiencing sonder for people who have already passed, I was feeling it. 

There was nothing that could prepare me for a small and unassuming chapel rose stone that had its top polished into a rectangle and  had on it three letters. Dad. This resonated with me more than anything I had seen. This man’s progeny honored him in private. Allowing strangers like me nothing to latch onto. 

The themes of religion and family were everywhere. I left the cemetery not with the macabre feeling I had when I entered, but rather a strong consanguinity with my fellow man. I continued my run under the non visible setting sun. Serving both as the end of the day and the metaphorical end to our class. The parallels one can draw from one day to one go around the seasons to one life was not lost on me. But what wisdom can I take from this investigation? This I did not know at the cemetery, but on the run I remembered a quote from The Denial Of Death. Ernest Becker writes “What does it mean to be a self-conscious animal? The idea is ludicrous, if it is not monstrous. It means to know that one is food for worms.” (Becker, 1974).
