Computational models of how people learn from wins and losses.

Reinforcement-learning models of the Iowa Gambling Task

preprint in preparation · code and full write-up on GitHub

The Iowa Gambling Task asks people to draw cards from four decks. Two decks pay well at first but lose money over time, and two pay less but come out ahead. The task is widely used to study decision-making, including in addiction and other clinical conditions. Computational models split each person’s choices into the processes behind them: how quickly they learn from wins and from losses, how strongly they stick with a deck, and how much they explore.

I implemented three standard models and fit them to 162 healthy adults, then tested a series of extensions, writing my predictions down before each analysis. The one that held up best restores outcome curvature to the ORL model: the felt size of a win or a loss grows more slowly than its dollar amount. ORL + α predicted held-out choices best of eight models, and on a second sample of 70 people, all five predictions written in advance were met.

No single model described everyone well, though. Different people were predicted best by different models, which led to the next project.

Mixing models: stacking

found in 162 · confirmed in a preregistered replication of 440

Picking one winning model throws away what the others get right. Stacking (Yao et al., 2018) keeps all of them: each choice gets a probability from a weighted average of the eight models’ predictions, with the weights chosen to predict held-out choices as well as possible. When one model is badly wrong about a choice, the others catch it.

One shared mix predicted held-out choices better than the best single model, ORL + α, for 124 of 162 people (z = 7.3). On average it gave the choice a person actually made about 4% more probability.

People also differ in their mix. Giving each person their own weights, pulled 70% of the way toward the shared mix, predicted better still (z = 4.3). The differences are real, but 100 choices are too few to pin down anyone’s mix exactly.

In a preregistered replication with 440 participants from the other studies in the data pool (registration), all ten predictions were met. The shared mix beat ORL + α for 328 of 440 people (z = 12.5), and personal weights pulled toward it predicted better still (z = 7.3). I call the whole procedure Seldon: predictions scored on held-out choices, the mix, the pull toward the group, and the tests of switching below.

Switching between strategies

found in 162 · confirmed in a preregistered replication of 440

A fixed mix assumes a person’s blend stays the same all session. A switching model lets one strategy be “in charge” of a run of choices before another takes over. It beat the fixed mix (z = 5.3), and it lost that edge when each person’s choices were shuffled, so the gain depends on the order of the choices. The best fit redrew the strategy in charge about once every 19 trials.

In the replication, switching again beat the fixed mix (z = 6.6) and again needed the choices in their real order (z = 6.4). On simulated players who never switch, the method found no switching, so the gain is not an artifact of the method. Two cautions from exploratory follow-ups: part of the gain comes from runs of near-random choice, lapses rather than changes of strategy, and with 100 choices the method cannot say exactly when a switch happens.

One model from two: NOVA

exploratory · predictions written in advance and met in the replication of 440

VSE says people are drawn back to decks they haven’t chosen in a while. ORL says they keep away from decks they just chose. These are the same term: VSE’s exploration bonus is mathematically identical to ORL’s perseverance with a negative weight. Drag VSE’s recovery rate: when it matches ORL’s decay, the two curves are the same curve.

So a model that combines them needs that term only once. NOVA (Novelty, Outcome curvature, Value decay, Alternation) adds to ORL + α the two parts it lacked: optimism about decks not yet tried, and decay of the values of decks left alone. Fit one person at a time, NOVA predicts no better than ORL + α: with eight parameters and 100 choices, its fits fall apart for some people. Fit with the group’s help, so each person’s settings are pulled toward the group’s, it beats ORL + α fit the same way (z = 6.4 in the replication of 440, predicted in advance). In the mix it is the most useful model: adding it improved the mix’s predictions (z = 5.4), and five models with NOVA beat all eight (z = 3.9), as predicted before the analysis was run. Even so, NOVA alone falls well short of the mix (z = −8.3), so one better model does not replace several.

Tiny recurrent neural networks

planned

Every model here builds in a learning rule. Tiny recurrent neural networks, with only a few units and no rule built in, can learn people’s choices directly, and they often predict those choices better than classic models do (Ji-An, Benna & Mattar, 2025). Trained and tested on the same held-out choices as the models, they will show how much predictable structure ORL + α and the stacked mix still miss, and the strategies the networks learn can point to what is missing. A second benchmark is Centaur, a language model fine-tuned on choices from 160 experiments (Binz et al., 2025), scored only on participants it never saw.

EEG: whose prediction errors does the brain track?

in preparation

With about 100 choices per person, behavior alone can only say so much about which strategy someone is using. Each model also makes a prediction error on every trial, and the brain’s response to feedback, the reward positivity, tracks prediction errors (Sambrook & Goslin, 2015). Testing whose prediction errors it follows, one model’s or a blend, would give neural evidence on strategy that choices alone cannot.

A first test can use open data: 59 people who did the task, 200 choices each, during EEG recording (Chávez-Sánchez et al., 2026), with the analysis preregistered before the EEG is examined. A study of my own would follow, with a longer task or two sessions so that each person makes more choices.
